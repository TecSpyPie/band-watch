Getting started

Band Watch shows which bands are open from where you are, using live reports of who is hearing whom (PSK Reporter). It can tune your radio, let you listen, decode FT8 and more, and keep your log.

  1. At the top, type your locator (grid square, like IO93) in Your locator.
  2. Type your callsign in Your callsign, so Band Watch can find reports of your own signal.
  3. The big line near the top tells you the best band right now. It updates every minute.

Connect your radio

Band Watch tunes your radio over its USB cable when you click a spot, the band scope, a shortwave station or the Radio panel, and follows your radio when you turn its dial. Close other programs that use the radio (WSJT-X, a logger) first.

Yaesu FT-991A (also FT-710, FTDX10, FTDX101)

  1. Install the Silicon Labs CP210x USB driver (on yaesu.com, on the radio's page), and plug the radio in.
  2. In Band Watch, Settings → Radio control: Radio make Yaesu, Baud rate 4800 (the FT-991A's menu 031 CAT RATE).
  3. Click Connect radio at the top and pick the Enhanced COM Port.

Icom IC-7300 (also IC-705, IC-7610, IC-9700)

  1. Install the CP210x USB driver from icomjapan.com (Support → Firmware/Drivers → IC-7300).
  2. On the radio: MENU → SET → Connectors → CI-V and set
    • CI-V USB Port: Unlink from [REMOTE]
    • CI-V USB Baud Rate: Auto
    • CI-V Transceive: ON (so Band Watch follows the radio's dial)
  3. In Band Watch, Settings → Radio control: Radio make Icom, Icom model IC-7300, Baud rate 115200.
  4. Click Connect radio and pick the Silicon Labs CP210x (or "USB to UART") port.

The IC-7300 has a sound card built in, so the FT8 decoder can take its audio straight from the USB cable: see Decoders.

ATS Mini, Malahit and Kenwood

  • ATS Mini: no driver. On the radio, Settings → USB Port → Ad hoc. Pick ATS Mini and click Connect radio. Only one program can use it at a time, so close ATS Mini Controller first.
  • Malahit DSP2: pick Malahit, click Connect radio and choose its Malahit CAT port (Windows may need the driver from malahiteam.com).
  • Kenwood (TS-590, TS-890, TS-990, TS-480, TS-2000): install Kenwood's COM port driver, pick Kenwood, and match the baud rate to the radio's menu.

The Radio panel

The front of a radio on your screen. It moves the same dial as the rest of Band Watch, so your radio and Listen follow it.

  • Tuning knob: drag it round with the mouse, scroll on it, or click it and use the arrow keys. Page Up and Page Down move ten steps. ◀ and ▶ move one step.
  • Step: how far one click of the knob moves (10 Hz to 10 kHz). Span: how wide the spectrum is.
  • Frequency: click the big numbers, type a frequency (14.2 for MHz or 7074 for kHz) and press Enter.
  • Band keys (160 to 6 m): go back to where you last were on that band, like a real radio.
  • Mode keys: LSB, USB, CW, AM, FM.
  • S-meter: live from the FT-991A (and other Yaesus), Icom radios and the ATS Mini.
  • Spectrum and waterfall: live from your RTL-SDR when you're listening with it, otherwise drawn from the stations spotted on that band. Click either to tune there.
  • Listen: starts and stops the receiver chosen in the Listen panel.
  • ATT (RTL-SDR): turns the dongle's RF gain down by 10, 20 or 30 dB, for very strong signals or a noisy band. With RF gain on Auto it counts down from full gain. The same switch is next to RF gain in Listen.
  • Receive controls (Yaesu and Icom radios, when connected): AF gain (the radio's speaker volume) and RF gain sliders, AGC (click to step FAST, MID, SLOW, AUTO), the preamp (IPO, AMP1, AMP2; on an Icom P.AMP), ATT, NB (noise blanker), DNR (noise reduction) and Auto notch. They follow the radio's own knobs within a few seconds.
  • Pop out ⧉: puts the radio, with Listen underneath, in its own window. Drag it onto another screen. Back in Band Watch brings it home.

The Radio panel only tunes and listens. Band Watch transmits only FT8 and FT4, only from the Decoder's Transmit box, and only while you have Enable TX switched on (it's off every time Band Watch starts).

Listen

Hear the band through a receiver on the internet or your own RTL-SDR.

  1. Pick a Receiver: the WebSDR at the University of Twente, another WebSDR or a KiwiSDR (type its address), or My RTL-SDR.
  2. Set the Band, Frequency and Mode, or just click a spot, the scope or the band plan.
  3. Click Listen. Click Stop to stop.

If you hear nothing from a web receiver, click inside it once: browsers need a click before they play sound.

Your RTL-SDR

Band Watch comes with rtl_tcp and starts it for you. Windows needs the dongle's driver changed once first, or no program can use it (even though Windows shows it's plugged in).

If the page says rtl_tcp is connected but not sending any signal, rtl_tcp has got stuck (it can after many hours running). If Band Watch started it, Band Watch restarts it for you (at most twice in 10 minutes). If you started it yourself, Band Watch leaves it alone: close it and start it again, or press Restart receiver to use Band Watch's own copy.

  1. Download Zadig from zadig.akeo.ie and run it.
  2. Click Options → List All Devices.
  3. Pick Bulk-In, Interface (Interface 0) (or RTL2838UHIDIR / RTL2832U). Not your keyboard, mouse or radio.
  4. Make sure the right-hand box says WinUSB, then click Replace Driver.
  5. Unplug the dongle, plug it back in, and restart Band Watch.
  6. In Listen, pick Receiver: My RTL-SDR and click Listen.

ClearRX: cleaner radio sound

ClearRX by M3VRV & King Coder is a radio audio processor for Windows that works with Band Watch. It's a program of its own, a separate download. It takes the sound from your radio and plays it back cleaned up: less hiss, no whistles, and a steady volume.

  1. Download ClearRX-Windows.zip from the latest release on GitHub (github.com/TecSpyPie/band-watch/releases/latest) and unzip it into C:\BandWatch\ClearRX. Double-click ClearRX.exe. (The next Band Watch update adds a desktop icon for it.)
  2. Under My radio, pick yours: RTL-SDR, Yaesu, Icom, ATS Mini, Malahit or Other / mixer. ClearRX finds its sound input, sets a good starting preset and shows a tip for that radio (for the RTL-SDR, the sound comes through Band Watch's Listen instead).
  3. Or, in Sound from, pick where your radio's sound comes in yourself: for the IC-7300 or FT-991A it's the radio's USB Audio CODEC; otherwise the PC's line-in, or your mixer or sound card.
  4. In Play to, pick your speakers or headphones (not the radio's USB sound, or you'd send it back to the radio).
  5. Press Start. The first time, allow ClearRX to use the sound input.
  6. Pick a preset: SSB voice for talking stations, Weak DX for faint ones, CW for Morse, AM broadcast for shortwave stations, or Off. Change any switch to make your own (it shows Custom).

The switches: Voice filter keeps only the speech frequencies; CW peak keeps just the Morse note (set the note to your radio's CW pitch); Auto notch removes steady whistles and carriers; Noise reduction takes away hiss (Strong can sound a little "underwater"); Steady volume evens out loud and quiet stations. Tick Hear it without ClearRX to compare. The spectrum shows the sound before (grey) and after (blue).

The extras (ClearRX 1.2.0), which stay as they are when you pick a preset: speed next to Steady volume sets how quickly it recovers after a loud signal (Fast, Medium, Slow); Noise blanker silences clicks and crackles such as electric fences and ignition (Strong catches more); Notch at removes a whistle at the frequency you choose; Squelch keeps quiet between signals (-70 dB opens easily, -30 dB only for strong ones); Bass and Treble lift or cut the low and high ends. Picking Off switches these off too.

With Band Watch: while ClearRX is open, Listen shows Send my RTL-SDR sound through ClearRX. Tick it and your RTL-SDR's sound is cleaned up with ClearRX's settings; change them in the ClearRX window. Band Watch works just the same when ClearRX is closed.

If the sound coming in is too loud, ClearRX says so: turn down the radio's USB audio level (on the IC-7300, MENU > SET > Connectors > ACC/USB AF Output Level) or the Volume in ClearRX. If Windows says it blocked ClearRX.exe, that's Smart App Control: ClearRX isn't signed yet. Band Watch works without it.

Bands, map and band scope

  • Bands from your location: each band shows Open, Marginal or Closed, from how many stations heard or were heard from your area in the last 30 minutes. Click a band to see it on the map and the scope.
  • Map: the "40 m spots" panel (it follows the band you pick) is a real world map, with no internet map service behind it. You are the ringed dot; every station on that band is a dot with a curved path to it, the way the radio wave goes. Colour and size show the signal (grey: no report, then Weak, Fair, Good, Strong) and a path fades as its spot gets older (spots older than 30 minutes drop off). The shaded side is night, with the Sun marked. Rest the pointer on a dot (or tap it) to see the callsign, distance, beam heading, signal and mode; click it for the station window. Turn the mouse wheel or use the + and − buttons (or pinch) to zoom, drag to move the map, double-click or press ⌂ to see the whole world again. Satellites, POTA activators and APRS stations show here too when you switch them on. Bowl is the older, simpler chart without coastlines; your choice is remembered. It works in the map's own window (⧉) too. The map uses the spots Band Watch already has, so it asks PSK Reporter for nothing extra.
  • 2 m and 70 cm: the band lists, activity table, spots map and band scope include 2 m and 70 cm, with spots from PSK Reporter (FT8 is on 144.174 and 432.174 MHz). They use the same spots already fetched every 5 minutes, so nothing extra is asked of PSK Reporter. The UK band plan has both bands too: CW and beacons, narrow band and FT8, satellites, FM simplex (calling 145.500 and 433.500), repeater inputs and outputs, APRS (144.800 and 433.800) and digital voice and hotspot areas. It is a simplified summary of the RSGB plan: check the RSGB plan and your licence before you transmit.
  • Band scope: where on the band the activity is. With an RTL-SDR, click Start live scope for a live spectrum and waterfall. Click anywhere on it to tune there.

Shortwave stations on air

Broadcasters on the air right now, from the free EiBi schedule.

  1. Pick a band (long wave, medium wave, 120 m to 11 m) or a language, or type a station or country.
  2. Tick Also starting in the next hour to see what's coming up.
  3. Click a frequency to tune there in AM. Your ATS Mini or radio follows.

The 49 m and 41 m bands are busiest after dark.

Decoders: FT8, FT4, WSPR, CW

Band Watch can decode what your receiver hears, like WSJT-X. Your PC's clock must be right to within a second.

  1. Pick the Mode: FT8, FT4, WSPR, CW or RTTY (see RTTY).
  2. Pick Audio from: My RTL-SDR, or This computer's sound input for a radio's audio (the IC-7300's USB sound card, or a cable from the radio's speaker).
  3. Tune to the right frequency (click a band key in the Radio panel, then pick DATA/USB), and click Start decoding.
  4. Tick CQ only to see just the stations calling CQ. Log on a line puts that station in the logbook.
  5. Results window opens the decodes in a window of their own.
  6. Columns and time zone (above the table): choose the columns, including Frequency (on the air, in kHz), and show times in UTC, your local time or any time zone. Drag a heading's edge to make a column wider. The Results window follows the same choices.

Licences and exams (UK)

In the UK there are three amateur licences: Foundation, Intermediate and Full. Each step up brings more bands, more power and more freedom. You pass Foundation before Intermediate, and Intermediate before Full (there's also a "Direct to Full" exam for people who already know the technical side).

  • The exams are taken online, usually at home with a remote invigilator watching over your webcam, or at a club. You book and pay online and choose a date and time that suits you.
  • The Intermediate exam is 46 multiple-choice questions in 90 minutes, and you see your result as soon as you finish. A course isn't required, but it's strongly recommended: there are online ones.
  • After you pass, the RSGB sends your certificate (in about a week). Then you apply on Ofcom's licensing site and choose your new callsign. Applying online is free.

Links (from the RSGB, the UK's national radio society):

  • Getting licensed and moving up (Foundation, Intermediate, Full)
  • The Intermediate licence and exam
  • Course and exam finder (courses and clubs near you, and online)
  • Training resources and syllabus videos
  • Exam questions answered (fees, sample papers, learning difficulties)

A club is a great help with any of this, and a friendly way to meet other amateurs.

Good operating: a code of practice

The good habits that make the bands a pleasant place for everyone. Your licence conditions always come first: this is the friendly part on top.

  1. Listen first. Before you call, listen for a while, then ask "Is this frequency in use?" (on CW: QRL?). On FT8, look at the waterfall and pick a clear spot.
  2. Say who you are. Give your callsign at the start and end of each contact and regularly in between, as your licence requires. Don't transmit without identifying.
  3. Keep to the band plan. CW in the CW parts, data in the data parts, phone where phone belongs. Band Watch shows the UK band plan and won't transmit data in the wrong segments.
  4. Use only the power you need. Less power, less interference, and more fun when it works. Never go above your licence's limit.
  5. Keep your signal clean. Set your audio level so the ALC only just moves (FT8, SSTV, CW from Band Watch), don't overdrive SSB, and check your signal isn't splattering.
  6. Be patient and polite. Wait your turn in a pile-up, don't call over a station working someone else, and answer "QRZ?" only when it's for you. Everyone was new once: help newcomers.
  7. Keep contacts friendly. Avoid arguments; leave politics, religion and bad language off the air.
  8. Don't cause trouble on purpose. No deliberate interference, no music or broadcasting, no messages in code you're not allowed to use.
  9. Know when to give way. Emergency traffic always comes first; keep 3760 kHz and other emergency centres clear unless you're helping.
  10. Never leave a transmitter unattended unless your licence allows it for that kind of station. With Band Watch: Enable TX goes off by itself, and Stop TX or Esc stops at once.
  11. Log your contacts and send QSLs when you can: it's part of the fun, and it's the record of what you did.
  12. Keep learning. Try a new mode, a new band, a club net, a contest, a satellite: there's always more.

CW keyboard

With the decoder's Mode on CW (Morse), the CW keyboard is under the decoded text. Type, and each letter is sent in Morse as you type it; you hear it and see its dots and dashes.

  • Practice (how it starts) plays to your speakers only. Choose a Speed, and Farnsworth spacing to learn: the letters at full speed, the gaps between them longer.
  • On the air sends real CW: connect your radio, put it in DATA-USB on a CW frequency (Band Watch sends a clean keyed tone into its USB sound), set the level with Tune, and switch Enable TX on. The radio goes back to receive 0.8 s after your last letter. Stop or Esc stops at once.
  • The buttons send CQ, a Call, a Report and 73 with your callsign, theirs (type it in Their call) and your locator filled in. Change them under Change the messages. Prosigns are written in angle brackets: <SK>, <KN>, <AR>, <BT>.

SSTV pictures

Set the decoder's Mode to SSTV (pictures), tune to an SSTV frequency (Tune to SSTV: 14.230 MHz USB on 20 m, 7.171 MHz LSB on 40 m; the ISS uses 145.800 MHz FM during its SSTV events) and Start decoding.

  • A picture starts by itself when its VIS code is heard, and builds line by line. Modes: Martin M1/M2, Scottie S1/S2/DX, Robot 36/72, PD 50/90/120/160/180/240/290, Wraase SC2-180 and Pasokon P3/P5/P7.
  • Band Watch follows each line's sync pulse, so a picture comes out straight even when a sound card runs a little fast or slow.
  • Missed the start? Pick the mode and click Start now: Band Watch finds the lines from their sync pulses. Stop picture keeps what has arrived.
  • The Gallery keeps your last 16 pictures in this browser. Click one to see it big; Save downloads it as a PNG.

Sending a picture

  1. Open Send a picture. Choose a Picture (or Use the picture above), the Mode, and the text for the top and bottom. Your callsign must be on the picture. The preview is exactly what will be sent.
  2. Click Tune to SSTV with your radio connected (DATA-USB, or DATA-LSB below 10 MHz), and set the level with Tune as for FT8.
  3. Switch Enable TX on, then Send picture. The red bar counts down; Stop TX or Esc stops at once. Enable TX goes off again after each picture.

Transmitting FT8 and FT4

With FT8 or FT4 as the decoder's mode, a Transmit box appears. Band Watch keys your radio over its USB cable (Yaesu, Icom or Kenwood) and plays the signal into the radio's USB sound, like WSJT-X.

  1. Set up once: connect your radio (Connect radio), and set your callsign and locator. In Sound to the radio, choose the radio's USB sound (FT-991A: USB Audio CODEC).
  2. Click Tune to FT8: the radio goes to the FT8 frequency in DATA-USB (so it takes its sound from the cable, not the microphone). Start decoding.
  3. Set the level: turn the radio's power down, click Tune (a steady tone for 10 s) and raise Level until the radio's ALC meter only just moves, then back it off a little. Too much level makes a dirty, wide signal.
  4. Make a QSO: click Reply on a CQ (or Call CQ), then Enable TX. Band Watch sends your grid, the report, RR73 and 73 as the other station answers, and stops when the QSO is done. Log QSO fills in the logbook; Band Watch also fills it in by itself when the QSO completes.

Safety: nothing is sent unless Enable TX is on, and it's off every time Band Watch starts. Stop TX or the Esc key stops at once. Band Watch won't transmit outside the amateur bands, stops after 6 overs with no answer (10 CQs) and unkeys the radio at the end of every over. Your power, and where in each band your licence allows you to transmit, are for you to set on the radio.

RTTY (radioteletype)

RTTY is typing sent as two audio tones, one for each of the two states (mark and space). Band Watch only receives it: it never transmits RTTY. The usual amateur RTTY is 45.45 baud with a 170 Hz shift, 5-bit Baudot (ITA2) letters and figures. Contests and DX use it a lot.

  1. Set the decoder's Mode to RTTY (radioteletype). The RTTY settings appear only for this mode.
  2. Tune the radio to a signal. Typical places: 14.080 to 14.099 MHz on 20 m (7.040 to 7.050 MHz on 40 m; contests spread out). Use the radio's LSB for RTTY (the amateur convention) or its DATA/FSK mode, with the tones near 2125 and 2295 Hz.
  3. Choose Audio from (your RTL-SDR, or the radio's sound), then click Start decoding.
  4. Click Find the tones while a signal is coming in, or type the Lower tone in Hz (the standard pair is 2125 Hz and 2295 Hz). The line under the settings says which tone is mark and which is space, and whether a signal is heard.
  5. The text appears in the box, and scrolls. Clear empties it.
  • Reverse swaps mark and space. If you get nonsense from a clear signal, tick it. On USB the sidebands are the wrong way round for the usual RTTY, so tick Reverse when you listen in USB.
  • Speed and shift are 45.45 baud and 170 Hz for amateur RTTY. Also offered: 50 and 75 baud, and 425, 450 and 850 Hz shift. To try it now: the German weather service (DWD) sends RTTY all day on 10,100.8 kHz, 7,646 kHz and 4,583 kHz at 50 baud and 450 Hz shift (listen on USB about 1.8 kHz below, then Find the tones).
  • Unshift on space (USOS) is on by default: after a space, the next character is read as a letter again, as most amateur software sends it. If the numbers after a space come out as letters, untick it.
  • Short bursts of garbage before and after a signal are normal. Poor signals lose letters; that's RTTY.

Antenna A/B: compare two antennas

This panel compares two antennas (or the same antenna before and after a change) using what other stations actually heard of your own WSPR signal. It only reads reports: it never controls your radio and never transmits. You send WSPR with your own software and switch antennas yourself. Your callsign (top of the page) must be set.

  1. Give the antennas names (say Dipole and Vertical). Start sending WSPR on the first one with your usual WSPR software.
  2. Press Now on antenna A when you start. When you swap, press Now on antenna B, and so on. Each press ends the last period and starts a new one. Stop logging ends it. Forgot to press? Use Add a period by typing the times (UTC), and delete any wrong period from the list.
  3. Swap often: every 10 to 20 minutes, A then B then A then B, for a few hours. Band and ionosphere conditions change all the time, so one long run on A and then one on B proves little.
  4. Pick where the reports come from: WSPR (the default), FT8 and FT4, or Both. Press Compare. For WSPR, Band Watch asks wspr.live (a free public WSPR database) for the reports of your callsign: one request per click, 7 days at most. For FT8 and FT4 it asks PSK Reporter through Band Watch's own server, which asks PSK Reporter at most once every 5 minutes per callsign and only covers the last 24 hours. Send FT8 or FT4 with your usual software (an FT8 over is 15 seconds, FT4 7.5 seconds) and swap antennas as above. For FT8 and FT4, keep Band Watch open while you alternate antennas. PSK Reporter only gives the latest report from each station, so Band Watch collects them for you: while a period is being logged, FT8 or FT4 is chosen and the page is showing, it asks every 5 minutes (and once when you press the antenna button) and saves what it gets in this browser (7 days at most). Compare uses everything collected, so matches build up as stations hear you on each antenna. Clear FT8 history empties it. A tab that is hidden or closed collects nothing.

How it is made fair. Only a receiving station that heard you on both antennas, on the same band, is compared. For each, Band Watch takes its average signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) on A and on B, then averages the differences. A report counts only if its whole slot (2 minutes for WSPR, 15 seconds for FT8, 7.5 seconds for FT4) is inside one period, so a swap in the middle never muddies it. Stations that heard only one antenna are counted but left out. WSPR and FT8/FT4 reports are never mixed: with Both, each band has a row for each source, and the Source column says which.

  • The table shows each band, how many receivers heard both, the average SNR for each antenna and the difference, for example Vertical better by 2.3 dB. About 6 dB is one S-unit; under 1 dB is hard to tell from chance.
  • By direction (8 compass sectors from your locator) and by distance show where one antenna does better, for example a vertical often wins far away and a low dipole nearby. Bars to the right favour B, to the left favour A.
  • With fewer than 10 receivers matched, Band Watch warns you that the result is shaky. Keep going and compare again.

Use the same power, mode and time of day on both antennas. WSPR reports come from wspr.live, FT8 and FT4 reports from PSK Reporter. FT8 and FT4 stations report you only if they run PSK Reporter reporting, so the count can differ from WSPR.

Parks on the air (POTA)

Activators on the air from parks right now, refreshed every two minutes.

  • Click a frequency to tune there; Log fills in the logbook with the park.
  • Filter by band or mode. Tick Tell me about new activators for an alert.

Wanted alerts

  1. Type the callsigns or prefixes you want (like VK* for all of Australia, or a full callsign).
  2. Type grid squares you want (like PM95, or QF for a whole field).
  3. Band Watch tells you when one is heard in the spots, your decodes or POTA. Tick Play a sound for a beep.

Logbook

  1. Fill in the Callsign (and anything else you like), or click Log on a decode or a POTA activator.
  2. Use Listen's frequency fills in the frequency and mode. Click Log it.
  3. Export ADIF saves your log for LoTW, Club Log or another logger; Import ADIF loads one in.
  4. Send to QRZ: paste your QRZ Logbook API key (on QRZ.com: Logbook → Settings) to send contacts to QRZ.

The log is kept in this browser on this PC. Export ADIF now and then as a backup.

My repeaters (2 m and 70 cm)

  • Your own list. Type in the repeaters you use: callsign, output frequency (what the repeater sends, which you listen to), shift, CTCSS tone or DCS/colour code, mode (FM, DMR, C4FM or D-STAR), a locator or town and notes. Only 2 m (144–146 MHz) and 70 cm (430–440 MHz) are accepted. The list is kept in this browser. Band Watch comes with no repeater list and doesn't fetch one.
  • Nearest first. Give a repeater a locator and Band Watch shows the distance and beam heading from your locator (the one at the top of the page) and sorts the nearest to the top.
  • Tune sets your connected radio (with the radio following Band Watch) or your RTL-SDR to the repeater's output in FM, so you can listen. It is listening only: Band Watch never sets the shift or tone on your radio, and never transmits. For a DMR, C4FM or D-STAR repeater, Band Watch can't decode the voice: set the mode on your radio (the Digital voice add-on can put a Yaesu on C4FM).
  • Import a CSV you downloaded. Choose a file and Band Watch shows how many repeaters it found before adding them. It reads columns called Callsign, Output or TX (the repeater's transmitter), Input or RX, Offset or Shift, CTCSS or Tone, Mode, Locator and Town. The file is read in your browser and never uploaded. Only import lists you're allowed to use for your own station.
  • Find repeaters: buttons open ukrepeater.net, Hearham and RepeaterBook in a new tab. Band Watch sends them nothing.

Band activity and callsign lookup

  • Band activity lists every report: who, where, which band and how strong. Rest the pointer on a callsign to see who it is; click it for its station window. Click a frequency to tune there.
  • Callsign lookup: type a callsign and click Look up. For names and addresses, click Log in to QRZ at the top with your own QRZ account.

ATS Mini

With the ATS Mini connected, this panel shows its screen live, with a big digital readout and an S-meter, and has its buttons: Band −/+, ◀ Tune / Tune ▶, Push, Mode, Volume and Light. Clicking a shortwave station or the Radio panel tunes it too.

On the radio, set Settings → USB Port → Ad hoc first. If the screen stays black, untick Live screen for a drawn screen instead.

Own windows and a second screen

Most parts have a ⧉ button by their name. It moves that part into a window of its own, which you can drag onto another screen and resize. Band Watch remembers where you put it. Close the window, or click Back to the main window, to put it back.

Settings and updates

  • Settings at the top has the display (colours, text size, layout and which parts to show), radio control, the RTL-SDR receiver, and profiles.
  • Updates: Settings → Updates → Install update gets the newest Band Watch and installs it over the old one. Your settings and log are kept.
  • Colours: Light, Dark or "Match my device" have the 3D look; High contrast and Night stay plain for easy reading.
  • Look: Settings → Look changes the style. Glass and depth puts frosted-glass panels over a soft background (your own background picture shows through, blurred), with the title bar, section menu, buttons and pop-up windows raised above them. It follows your device's "reduce transparency" and "reduce motion" settings.

Section menu and folding. The bar under the title has a button for each part that is showing (Clock, Radio panel, Listen, Decoders, POTA and so on). Click one to glide to that part; the button for the part you are looking at is highlighted. On a narrow screen the bar slides sideways. Each part also has a small arrow by its name: click it to fold the part so only its name shows, and again to unfold it. Folding only hides the part, so decoders, your radio and Listen keep working. Folded parts stay folded next time, and show in the menu with a dashed outline; clicking one unfolds it. If you don't want the bar, untick Settings → Show the section menu.

    If something doesn't work

    • "Windows protected your PC": click More info, then Run anyway. Band Watch isn't signed with a paid certificate yet.
    • The radio won't connect: check its USB driver is installed, close other programs that use it, and look at what Settings → Radio control says. Only Chrome and Edge can talk to radios.
    • The RTL-SDR isn't found: do the Zadig step.
    • No sound: click inside the web receiver once; check the volume; in the Radio panel's own window, press Listen there.
    • The website version is blank at first: it sleeps when nobody is using it. Wait a few seconds and reload.
    • Nothing new on the bands: Band Watch only shows what stations report. Quiet bands, or a wrong locator, show less.
